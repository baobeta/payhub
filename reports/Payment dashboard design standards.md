# Payment dashboards converge on one defensible blueprint

The seven platforms studied (Stripe, Adyen, Checkout.com, Braintree/PayPal, Square, Razorpay, Xendit) build their merchant dashboards to largely the same design. The navigation groups Home, Money, Payments, Developers and Settings. The payment detail page is built around a **chronological event timeline**. Refunds, captures and voids are **contextual, reason-required modals**. Team access uses **function-named roles** that separate granting access from moving money. API keys **carry their environment in a prefix**, are **shown once**, and are **rotated with an overlap window**. Test data lives in environments that never share objects or keys with live. Login is gated by **mandatory or admin-enforced TOTP-class 2FA**, and sensitive team activity goes to an exportable **security history**. None of these choices is arbitrary. Each one follows a PCI DSS v4.0 requirement (Req 7 least privilege, Req 8 authentication, Req 10 audit), a SOC 2 common criterion, or a developer-experience lesson such as zero-downtime key rotation. Internal operator consoles are almost undocumented in public. The fintechs that do write about them (Monzo, Modern Treasury, Pigment) describe the same controls: multi-party authorisation for sensitive actions, compensating ledger entries instead of edits, and read-only, time-boxed, visibly flagged impersonation. For payhub, the "market standard, not enterprise" line falls in a clear place. Adopt the timeline, the role split, prefixed and hashed keys with test/live separation and rolling rotation, TOTP with lockout and idle timeout, an append-only security history, and a maker-checker operator console. Skip custom roles, SSO/SCIM, organizations, multiple sandboxes, IP access policies and an audit API.

## Every dashboard organises around money, payments and a separate developer area

Stripe's sidebar shows the layout the others share. **Home** carries analytics and alerts such as unresolved disputes. **Balances** covers payouts and transaction history. **Transactions** is a filterable, exportable list of all payments. Customers and the product catalog come next. Settings is split into Personal, Account and Product, with "Team and security" under Account ([Stripe Web Dashboard](https://docs.stripe.com/dashboard/basics)). Developer tooling sits in its own area. Stripe's Workbench "logs every successful or failed request made using your API keys", including the request, the response and links to related resources ([Stripe Web Dashboard](https://docs.stripe.com/dashboard/basics)). Adyen and Braintree make the same separation through permissions: their role categories (Account, Developer, Finance, Risk, Transactions at Adyen; Transactions, Fraud Tools, Webhooks, User Management at Braintree) follow their navigation areas one-for-one ([Adyen User roles](https://docs.adyen.com/account/user-roles/); [Braintree Role Permissions](https://developer.paypal.com/braintree/articles/control-panel/users-roles/role-permissions)). The reason is least privilege turned into navigation. A Support or Dispute role never needs request logs or keys, and a Developer never needs bank settings. When navigation follows role boundaries, whole sections can be hidden instead of showing disabled buttons.

The payment detail page is where the dashboards agree most. Checkout.com shows "a full action timeline (most recent first)" that "shows the entire lifecycle, including its path from authorization to capture, void, or refund", with per-event proof PDFs and capture, void and refund actions available in the same panel ([Checkout.com Payment activity](https://www.checkout.com/docs/business-operations/use-the-dashboard/payment-activity)). Adyen's page shows "the history of different statuses that the payment has gone through", events that carry the ARN, the fraud score, whether 3D Secure applied, whether liability shifted, and fees. Shopper PII is **masked by default** and can be unmasked only by users with a dedicated Merchant view PII role, because unmasked PII "can affect your PCI DSS compliance level" ([Adyen Manage payments](https://docs.adyen.com/account/manage-payments); [Adyen User roles](https://docs.adyen.com/account/user-roles/)). Stripe's timeline makes each refund a child event with its own ARN/STAN for tracing with the bank, and lets staff add notes and @mention teammates on a payment ([Stripe Refunds](https://docs.stripe.com/refunds); [Stripe Start a team](https://docs.stripe.com/get-started/account/teams)). The pattern is a single timeline of immutable events, each with its own references and documents. It works because payments really are a sequence of asynchronous events, and a support agent's first question is always "what happened, in what order, and what did the network say".

Dangerous actions follow a consistent friction model. Stripe's refund modal **defaults to the full amount**, accepts a partial amount, requires a reason (and a note if "Other"), and refuses to refund more than the original charge in total. Bulk refunds are full-only: "partial refunds must be issued individually." Cancelling a refund needs an explicit "**Yes, cancel refund**" button ([Stripe Refunds](https://docs.stripe.com/refunds)). Adyen offers actions only in valid states (capture only when Authorised, refund only after capture) and requires a **typed confirmation code** to cancel, which is heavier friction reserved for an action that cannot be undone ([Adyen Manage payments](https://docs.adyen.com/account/manage-payments)). Stripe also steers users toward cancelling uncaptured authorisations instead of refunding them, which is cheaper and cleaner ([Stripe Refunds](https://docs.stripe.com/refunds)). The friction is graded. Routine reversible actions get a modal and a reason. Irreversible actions get typed confirmation. Batch actions get only the safest variant. The research found no public documentation of how dashboards prevent double-submitted refunds, so UI-side idempotency is a design choice payhub must make itself rather than copy.

## Roles split along four questions auditors ask

Every provider ships **predefined, job-named roles**, and they separate the same duties. Stripe's catalogue shows it most clearly. Its **IAM Administrator** can invite and edit members and view security history but "can't do anything beyond access management" and cannot grant Admin. **Developer** can create secret keys and refund, but cannot invite members or touch bank accounts or payout schedules. **Refund Analyst** "can't create payments, view balance, or view connected accounts". **Support Communications** "can't access financial information". **View Only** can read and export but not write ([Stripe User roles](https://docs.stripe.com/get-started/account/teams/roles)). Checkout.com has 14 predefined roles with the same IAM admin/operational admin split ([Checkout.com User permissions](https://www.checkout.com/docs/business-operations/use-the-dashboard/manage-users/user-permissions)). Razorpay adds a **Finance** role that "cannot refund or capture", a **View-Only (Auditor)** role whose page views are all logged, and a **Pseudo Owner** (at most two) "for business continuity" ([Razorpay Manage team](https://razorpay.com/docs/payments/dashboard/account-settings/manage-team/)). Xendit uses composable flags instead and separates **Edit from Approve** for payouts ([Xendit Users & permissions](https://docs.xendit.co/docs/users-permissions)).

Underneath the naming, each split answers one of four questions: who can **grant access**, who can **move money out** (payouts, bank accounts), who can **reverse money to customers** (refunds), and who can **see secrets** (API keys, unmasked PII). The PCI DSS v4.0 text requires this. **7.2.1** requires access based on "users' job classification and functions" with "the least privileges required", **7.2.4** requires reviewing every account's privileges "at least once every six months", and **7.3.3** requires "deny all" by default ([Microsoft Learn: PCI-DSS Requirement 7](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/standards/pci-requirement-7)). The providers also give concrete security reasons. Stripe warns that invite-capable roles are an account-takeover amplifier: "If an attacker compromises a user with one, they can invite additional users under their control." It also requires account-wide 2FA before non-admin Transfer Analysts can move funds ([Stripe User roles](https://docs.stripe.com/get-started/account/teams/roles)). Checkout.com keeps user management, SSO settings and processing settings **out of custom roles entirely** ([Checkout.com User permissions](https://www.checkout.com/docs/business-operations/use-the-dashboard/manage-users/user-permissions)).

Two design choices divide the market. The first is **single role per user versus additive roles**. Checkout.com ("A user cannot have multiple roles assigned") and Razorpay ("only one role") keep auditing simple. Stripe allows additive roles and warns about "conflicts and unintended authority" ([Stripe User roles](https://docs.stripe.com/get-started/account/teams/roles)). The second is **custom roles**, which are the enterprise differentiator. Checkout.com and Square's paid tiers offer them ([Square Permission sets](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/5822-employee-permissions)). Adyen composes around 13 categories of fine-grained roles into role groups instead ([Adyen User roles](https://docs.adyen.com/account/user-roles/)). Invites are email-based, the role is chosen at invite time, and they expire: Stripe's after **10 days**, with the guidance "Grant the lowest permission required by the user to perform their job" ([Stripe Start a team](https://docs.stripe.com/get-started/account/teams)). An organization layer above accounts (Stripe Organizations, Checkout.com sub-entities, Square locations) exists for multi-entity businesses and is plainly an enterprise feature ([Stripe Organizations](https://docs.stripe.com/get-started/account/orgs)).

## Keys name their environment, appear once, and roll without downtime

Every platform splits credentials into a **client-safe identifier that can only tokenize** and a **server-only secret**. Mature platforms add **scoped secrets** and keep **webhook signing secrets separate** from API keys. Stripe issues `pk_`, `rk_` (restricted) and `sk_` keys, each in `_test_` and `_live_` variants. It now says "we don't recommend using secret keys for new use cases" and recommends restricted keys instead. Webhook secrets are `whsec_` and are "per-webhook secrets", not API keys ([Stripe API keys](https://docs.stripe.com/keys)). Checkout.com uses `sk_sbox_`/`pk_sbox_` in sandbox ([Checkout.com API keys](https://www.checkout.com/docs/developer-resources/api/manage-api-keys/api-keys)). Xendit ships **zero secret keys by default** and gives each key None/Read/Write per product ([Xendit API keys](https://docs.xendit.co/docs/api-keys)). The prefixes have two purposes. Humans and code can tell test from live at a glance. And secret scanners can match keys with few false positives: GitHub's partner program asks for "a uniquely defined prefix" plus "high entropy random strings", and forwards leaked tokens to the issuer for revocation ([GitHub secret scanning partner program](https://docs.github.com/en/code-security/secret-scanning/secret-scanning-partnership-program/secret-scanning-partner-program)). Stripe says it "may invalidate [leaked keys] automatically" ([Stripe Support: compromised keys](https://support.stripe.com/questions/protecting-against-compromised-api-keys)). The key also decides the environment. Stripe states that the Dashboard's mode "doesn't affect your integration code. Your test and live mode API keys affect the behavior of your code" ([Stripe testing use cases](https://docs.stripe.com/testing-use-cases)).

The secret lifecycle is the same everywhere. **Show once.** Stripe says "Save the key value. You can't retrieve it later," and prompts the user to "Add a note" recording where the key was stored. Adyen says "You cannot copy the API key again after you leave the page." Razorpay's secret is "never displayed again" ([Stripe API keys](https://docs.stripe.com/keys); [Adyen API credentials](https://docs.adyen.com/development-resources/api-credentials/); [Razorpay authentication](https://razorpay.com/docs/api/authentication/)). **Step up before creating.** Stripe sends an email or SMS code, and Xendit asks for the password again ([Stripe API keys](https://docs.stripe.com/keys); [Xendit API keys](https://docs.xendit.co/docs/api-keys)). **Roll with overlap.** When a Stripe key is rotated, "both the old and new keys work for up to 7 days", and Stripe advises expiring the old key only after its request volume "has been at zero for a few hours or days", which it checks through a per-key "View request logs" link ([Stripe API keys](https://docs.stripe.com/keys)). Adyen's previous key stays active for **24 hours**. Adyen credentials "cannot be deleted but can be deactivated", so the record survives for audit ([Adyen API credentials](https://docs.adyen.com/development-resources/api-credentials/)). Razorpay offers immediate deactivation or a 24-hour rollover, and Xendit shows **"Last Used"** metadata so unused keys can be pruned ([Razorpay authentication](https://razorpay.com/docs/api/authentication/); [Xendit API keys](https://docs.xendit.co/docs/api-keys)). The main developer-experience point is that the overlap window turns rotation from an outage into an ordinary deploy, and usage visibility is what lets the developer close the window safely. No provider states publicly that secrets are stored hashed. Show-once is consistent with hashing but does not prove it.

Test and live separation comes in two models. In the **same account with a toggle** (Stripe's legacy test mode, Razorpay, Xendit), keys carry the environment. In **fully separate environments** (Adyen, Braintree, Square, PayPal), the login, dashboard and hostname are separate: Braintree says "The sandbox is an entirely separate environment from your production account" ([Braintree testing](https://developer.paypal.com/braintree/docs/reference/general/testing)). Stripe is moving from the first model toward the second. Its legacy test mode shares settings with live, and "If you change settings in the Dashboard while in the test mode sandbox, you might also change them in live mode". The new Sandboxes (up to five) isolate settings completely and support sandbox-only invites ([Stripe testing use cases](https://docs.stripe.com/testing-use-cases)). In both models, "objects in one mode aren't accessible to the other" ([Stripe API keys](https://docs.stripe.com/keys)), and webhook secrets differ per environment ([Stripe webhooks](https://docs.stripe.com/webhooks); [Adyen configure webhooks](https://docs.adyen.com/development-resources/webhooks/configure-and-manage/)). Every platform publishes **deterministic magic inputs**. Stripe uses card numbers (`4242…` succeeds, `4000000000000002` declines, `4000000000000259` disputes). Braintree uses amounts ($2,000–$2,999.99 is a processor decline). Adyen uses cardholder names such as `DECLINED` and `FRAUD`. Opaque tokens such as `pm_card_visa` let server tests avoid raw PANs "for PCI compliance" ([Stripe testing](https://docs.stripe.com/testing); [Braintree testing](https://developer.paypal.com/braintree/docs/reference/general/testing); [Adyen test result codes](https://docs.adyen.com/development-resources/testing/result-codes/)). The stated reason for separation is safety: test keys ensure "you don't accidentally create or modify live transactions" ([Xendit Help Center](https://help.xendit.co/hc/en-us/articles/16516398053273-What-is-API-Key-and-How-Do-I-Create-Them)).

Webhook management is the counterpart to key management. Stripe's endpoint UI reveals the signing secret on demand. It has a **Roll secret** action that can keep the old secret alive for up to 24 hours while Stripe "generates one signature per secret". Signatures cover `timestamp.payload` with a default 5-minute replay tolerance. An Event deliveries tab shows each event as Delivered, Pending or Failed, with the HTTP status and the next retry time. A manual **Resend** works for 15 days and "does not cancel automatic retries" ([Stripe webhooks](https://docs.stripe.com/webhooks)). Adyen adds a "Test configuration" button and a delivery event log ([Adyen configure webhooks](https://docs.adyen.com/development-resources/webhooks/configure-and-manage/)).

## Login security copies PCI's numbers even where PCI does not strictly apply

2FA is now the baseline. It is **mandatory** at Adyen ("All users… must set up Multifactor Authentication (MFA) or Single Sign-On"), at Braintree (since September 2023) and at Xendit ("Enabling 2FA is mandatory"). It is **admin-enforceable** at Stripe, Square and Razorpay ([Adyen MFA](https://docs.adyen.com/account/multifactor-authentication); [Braintree 2FA](https://developer.paypal.com/braintree/articles/risk-and-security/control-panel-security/two-factor-authentication); [Xendit 2FA](https://docs.xendit.co/dashboard/2-factor-authentication/); [Square 2-step verification](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/5593-2-step-verification)). TOTP is supported everywhere. Stripe ranks passkeys and security keys highest "because they're resistant to phishing" and says SMS "is vulnerable to SIM-swapping… use it only as a last resort" ([Stripe Security](https://docs.stripe.com/security)). Recovery codes are standard (Stripe, Xendit), and admins can reset a locked-out member's MFA (Checkout.com, Razorpay) ([Stripe 2FA](https://support.stripe.com/questions/enable-two-step-authentication); [Checkout.com MFA](https://www.checkout.com/docs/business-operations/use-the-dashboard/configure-multi-factor-authentication)). Razorpay locks a user out after 9 wrong OTPs ([Razorpay Manage team](https://razorpay.com/docs/payments/dashboard/my-account/manage-team/)). That sits inside PCI DSS **8.3.4**: lock out after at most 10 attempts, for at least 30 minutes ([PCI Compliance Hub](https://pcicompliancehub.com/blog/pci-dss-requirement-8-2-8-idle-timeout-reauthentication)). **8.4.2**, which requires MFA for all non-console access into the CDE, became mandatory on 31 March 2025 ([PCI SSC Summary of Changes](https://listings.pcisecuritystandards.org/documents/PCI-DSS-v3-2-1-to-v4-0-Summary-of-Changes-r1.pdf)), and **8.2.8** requires re-authentication after **15 minutes idle** ([PCI Compliance Hub](https://pcicompliancehub.com/blog/pci-dss-requirement-8-2-8-idle-timeout-reauthentication)). A merchant dashboard that shows only masked or tokenized card data usually falls outside the CDE, so for the merchant these numbers are defense in depth and SOC 2 CC6.1 evidence rather than a hard obligation. They become binding for the provider's own staff and for any screen that shows full PANs. No provider publicly documents its actual dashboard idle timeout.

SAML SSO is the enterprise tier. Stripe offers SAML with mandatory or optional SSO, JIT provisioning, IdP-mapped roles and SCIM. It notes that without SCIM a user removed from the IdP loses Stripe access only at their next SSO sign-in ([Stripe SSO](https://docs.stripe.com/get-started/account/sso)). Checkout.com and Adyen treat SSO as a *replacement* for their own MFA ([Checkout.com MFA](https://www.checkout.com/docs/business-operations/use-the-dashboard/configure-multi-factor-authentication)). Stripe groups MFA under "Sensitive action authentication" (step-up) and emails users about logins from unknown IPs or devices ([Stripe Security](https://docs.stripe.com/security)). No provider publishes a complete list of step-up-protected actions.

Audit history is where Stripe sets the benchmark. **Security history** keeps "account activity of team members during the past 180 days", covering "logging in or changing bank account information", and can be exported ([Stripe Start a team](https://docs.stripe.com/get-started/account/teams); [Stripe Security](https://docs.stripe.com/security)). Its 2026 **Activity Logs API** records `api_key_created/updated/deleted/viewed`, invite create, delete and accept events, and role changes tagged with their source (Dashboard, SCIM or SSO). Each entry has an actor, a timestamp, affected resources and metadata, and entries are kept for **6 months**. The API is positioned explicitly for SIEM ingestion and "SOC 2 or PCI DSS" ([Stripe Activity logs](https://docs.stripe.com/activity-logs)). Note that even *viewing* a key is an audited event. PCI DSS 10.2.1 defines what must be captured: individual access to cardholder data, all admin actions, invalid access attempts, and changes to authentication credentials. 10.5.1 requires **12 months of retention with 3 immediately available** ([RSI Security](https://blog.rsisecurity.com/tracking-and-monitoring-under-pci-dss-requirement-10/); [KirkpatrickPrice 10.5.1](https://explore.kirkpatrickprice.com/videos/pci-v4-0-10-5-1-retain-audit-log-history-for-at-least-12-months)). Stripe's 6 months falls short of that because 10.5.1 binds the entity's own CDE logs, not a customer feature. Merchants who need a year export the logs, so a product that keeps 12 months is above the market baseline. Outside Stripe, Adyen offers only feature-specific audit logs (terminals, capabilities), and no public documentation was found for Checkout.com, Braintree, Square or Xendit.

## Operator consoles use four-eyes control, reasons and compensating entries

The seven PSPs publish almost nothing about their internal consoles, so the evidence comes from adjacent fintechs, and several conclusions below are inferences. Monzo is the clearest primary source. "Sensitive actions by our customer operations team… require multi-party authorisation". Internal apps use RBAC "where each role is scoped to a particular responsibility", and a support specialist may hold "dozens of roles". Staff use hardware tokens partly to "enforce our policy on no credential sharing". Break-glass access triggers "very loud alerts" that page security, and keeps "the same level of audit scope" as normal access ([Monzo security post](https://monzo.com/blog/2022/03/31/how-we-secure-monzos-banking-platform)). Monzo's support tooling is case-centric: it "summaris[es] and highlight[s] the key facts of the case" and guides the agent's next step ([Monzo customer support design](https://monzo.com/blog/2020/11/11/customer-support-design)). Maker-checker, where a different person approves what the maker initiated, is the standard segregation-of-duties control for manual ledger corrections and large movements ([Wikipedia, Maker-checker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maker-checker)). No fintech primary source documents mandatory reason codes or ticket references in so many words, although merchant-side reason fields (Stripe refunds) and Monzo's case orientation point strongly toward them.

Exception handling is queue-driven, not silent. Uber marks events that fail reconciliation "as unhealthy and submits them to the DLQ" for human handling ([Uber settlement accounting](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/ubers-advanced-settlement-accounting-system/)). Stripe's merchant webhook UI (delivery status, next retry, bounded manual resend that leaves automatic retries in place) is the best public proxy for what an internal replay tool exposes ([Stripe webhooks](https://docs.stripe.com/webhooks)).

Ledger corrections are **never edits**. Modern Treasury's argument is that "if the data has been mutated, then the data is irreversibly destroyed and then becomes impossible to figure out what changed". It offers two corrections: a full reversal plus a new correct transaction, or a single difference transaction. Mutable business objects are kept separate from immutable ledger transactions, which remain "the ultimate source of truth" ([Modern Treasury, Enforcing Immutability](https://www.moderntreasury.com/journal/enforcing-immutability-in-your-double-entry-ledger)). Modern Treasury also notes a readability cost: reversal entries make it hard to "distinguish between debits that were client-initiated and debits that are generated by the ledger as reversals" ([Modern Treasury, Scaling a Ledger Part V](https://www.moderntreasury.com/journal/how-to-scale-a-ledger-part-v)). The fix is to label every operator-generated entry as such.

Impersonation has one detailed public design, from Pigment (not a fintech). It issues a separate token carrying *both* identities, a read-only flag and a **30-minute expiry**. Middleware rejects mutations with 403, and a build-time linter forces every endpoint to declare whether it is allowed under impersonation. Customers can revoke impersonation permission themselves. Every session start is logged, and a dark border frames the screen during impersonation ([Pigment Engineering](https://engineering.pigment.com/2026/04/08/safe-user-impersonation/)). Applied to payments, a "view as merchant" feature should be read-only and time-boxed, and any write should go through the normal audited operator path, never through the impersonated session. That is an inference, combining Pigment's pattern with PCI 10.2.1's per-user access logging.

## Recommendations for payhub: what to build, what to skip

payhub's existing primitives already match the market pattern: an append-only double-entry ledger, an `unknown` state with only three governed exits, outbound webhook redelivery, and hashed `sk_live_` keys. The UI's job is to *expose* those guarantees without weakening them. The tables below state each recommendation and its reason. Where a recommendation goes beyond the cited sources, it is marked as payhub-specific judgment.

### Merchant area

| Adopt | Why |
|---|---|
| Sidebar of **Home / Payments / Developers (API keys, Webhooks, Request log) / Settings (Team, Security, Security history)**, with each section hidden unless the role grants it | Mirrors Stripe/Adyen and turns least privilege into navigation. Hiding sections avoids disabled-button clutter. |
| Payment detail built on **one timeline** of state transitions, PSP attempts, ledger postings and webhook deliveries, newest first, each with its own references. `unknown` shown as a first-class event explaining *why* (for example a PSP timeout) and which exits are pending (poll, webhook, operator) | The timeline is the universal organising element (Checkout.com, Adyen, Stripe). It is also the most direct way to show payhub's `unknown` design, which is the demo's differentiator. |
| Card data shown as brand + last 4 only, with **no PAN anywhere** and customer email/phone masked by default | Keeps payhub out of CDE scope, which is why Adyen gates unmasking behind a separate role. For a demo, "never show" is simpler than an unmask role. |
| Refund / capture / void as **state-gated modals**: full amount prefilled, remaining-refundable cap, required reason (note when "Other"), named confirm button, amounts entered in currency units and sent as minor units, and a **client-generated idempotency key per modal instance** | Stripe/Adyen patterns. The idempotency key is payhub-specific judgment, since the sources do not say how dashboards stop double submits, and payhub already needs idempotent APIs. |
| **Five fixed roles, one per member**: Owner, Admin, Developer, Support (refund + read), View only. Owner-only for team management and ownership transfer. Invites expire after 10 days | Matches the four-question split (grant access / move money / reverse money / see secrets). Single-role is easier to audit (Checkout.com, Razorpay) and avoids Stripe's "unintended authority" warning. |
| **Test and live as separate key prefixes and separate data**: add `sk_test_` beside `sk_live_`, scope every object to an environment column, and show a persistent mode indicator | Key-carried environment is the Stripe/Razorpay/Xendit model and costs one column. The shared-settings problem that pushed Stripe to Sandboxes does not arise if payhub has little per-mode configuration. |
| **Keys stored as a digest, shown once**, with a list view showing the prefix + last 4, creator, created_at, a free-text "where stored" note and **last_used_at** | Show-once plus Stripe's note field plus Xendit's "Last Used" is the market set. payhub's single SHA-256 digest is sufficient for high-entropy random keys (payhub-specific judgment: slow hashes guard low-entropy passwords, not 256-bit tokens). The UI needs the non-secret prefix/last 4 stored alongside it. |
| **Move keys to a table of many rows per merchant**, so **roll** creates a new key and sets `expires_at` on the old one (choice of now / 24h / 7 days), and **revoke** expires immediately but keeps the row | A single digest column cannot support overlap. Rolling with overlap (Stripe 7d, Adyen 24h) is what makes rotation safe, and "deactivate, not delete" (Adyen) keeps the audit trail. |
| **Step-up re-authentication** (TOTP or password) before key create/roll, member invite or role change, and disabling 2FA | Stripe/Xendit step up on key creation. These are the PCI 10.2.1.2/10.2.1.5 high-risk events. |
| Webhooks page: endpoint list, secret reveal and **roll with 24h dual-signing**, and a delivery log showing status, HTTP code, attempt count, next retry and **Resend** (which does not cancel automatic retries) | Stripe's design, and payhub already has redelivery. Dual-signing applies if payhub signs outbound webhooks. |
| **Mandatory TOTP with recovery codes** for all dashboard users, lockout after 10 failures for 30 minutes, a 15-minute idle timeout, and an email on sign-in from a new IP/device | Mandatory is the Adyen/Braintree/Xendit norm and simpler than an admin toggle. The numbers are PCI 8.3.4/8.2.8 and cost almost nothing to adopt. |
| **Security history** as an append-only `audit_events` table (actor, action, target, IP, user agent, result, timestamp, per PCI 10.2.2 fields), readable by Owner/Admin, **CSV export**, 12-month retention | Stripe's Security history is the benchmark. 12 months beats Stripe's 6 and matches PCI 10.5.1. It reuses the append-only discipline payhub already applies to its ledger. |

|---|---|
| Custom roles, role groups, additive multi-role | Enterprise differentiator (Checkout.com, Square paid tiers). Fixed roles already demonstrate least privilege. |
| SAML SSO, SCIM, JIT provisioning | Enterprise tier. They mainly solve IdP-driven deprovisioning, which a demo with a few seeded users does not need. |
| Organizations, account switcher, entity scoping | Only multi-entity businesses need them. They add a whole tenancy layer. |
| Multiple isolated sandboxes | Stripe's answer to shared-settings pain at scale. One test environment is enough. |
| Restricted/scoped keys with per-resource permissions | Now Stripe's default, but it requires a permission matrix across every endpoint. Worth mentioning in docs as the next step, not building. |
| Publishable keys | Useful only if payhub offers client-side tokenization. It does not appear to, so skip until it does. |
| API key IP access policies, audit-log API/SIEM feed, dormant-key auto-restriction | Enterprise operational features. CSV export covers the audit need for a demo. |
| Passkeys/WebAuthn | Best practice, but more build effort than TOTP. A reasonable follow-up, not a v1 requirement. |

### Operator area

| Adopt | Why |
|---|---|
| **Separate area with separate authentication** (distinct operator model and session, mandatory TOTP, shorter idle timeout) that calls the same domain services as the API, never direct model writes | Staff access is separated from customer auth at Monzo. Routing through services keeps the state machine and ledger invariants in force (inferred practice). |
| **Needs-attention queues** as the home page: payments in `unknown` (with age), webhook deliveries that exhausted retries, and ledger/reconciliation breaks | Exception queues rather than silent fixes (Uber DLQ). This also shows payhub's `unknown` design working. |
| Cross-merchant payment search and a **superset payment detail** with raw PSP request/response payloads (redacted) and webhook attempts | An internal console is a superset of the merchant view. The raw payload is what support needs to resolve `unknown`. |
| **Governed exits from `unknown`**: "Poll PSP now" as the primary button. A manual transition requires a reason code, free text and a case reference, and must be **approved by a second operator** (maker ≠ checker) before it is applied | Monzo's multi-party authorisation for sensitive ops actions. A manual transition out of `unknown` moves money, just like a ledger adjustment. |
| **Ledger corrections as proposals**: an operator drafts a balanced compensating transaction (reversal + re-post, or a delta) linked to the original, with a reason; a different operator approves; the posted entries are tagged `source: operator` | Modern Treasury's never-edit rule, plus a source label to solve its readability concern. payhub's append-only ledger already enforces the "never edit" half. |
| Webhook redelivery from the console, with a reason, audited, leaving automatic retries untouched | Mirrors Stripe's resend semantics. The capability already exists and only needs a governed UI. |
| **Read-only "view as merchant"**: 30-minute expiry, case reference required, a banner/border on every screen, the operator identity carried on every request, start/stop audited, and all mutations rejected server-side | Pigment's design. Writes go through the audited operator path instead. |
| Operator roles: **Support** (read, poll, resend), **Ops** (propose transitions/adjustments), **Finance approver** (approve), **Operator admin** (manage operators, no approvals) | Separates granting access from moving money, as the merchant roles do. It also makes maker-checker enforceable by role and by the "not yourself" rule. |
| One **operator audit stream** with a "who has which role" export | PCI 7.2.4 six-monthly reviews imply this report. It is cheap to add given the audit table. |

| Skip (enterprise-only) | Why |
|---|---|
| Break-glass with paging, JIT time-boxed elevation, hardware-token-only MFA | Banking-grade infrastructure controls (Monzo). TOTP plus maker-checker demonstrates the principle. |
| Merchant-side consent toggle for impersonation, "recent access" view for merchants | Good practice (Pigment), but it needs a merchant settings surface and notification plumbing. Log impersonation now and expose it later. |
| Amount thresholds for when a second approver is required | No public benchmarks exist. Requiring approval on every money-moving manual action is simpler and stricter for a demo. |
| Case/ticketing system | Store a free-text case reference instead of integrating a helpdesk. |

### Public demo area

Adopt a **test-mode-only** public area that uses deterministic magic inputs in the style of Stripe card numbers or Braintree amounts. At minimum there should be inputs that succeed, decline, time out into `unknown` and resolve by webhook, and time out and require an operator. Label the environment prominently, never accept or display real card numbers, and offer a "reset demo data" action like Stripe's "Delete test data" and Braintree's "Purge Test Data". Skip self-serve signup to live mode: Razorpay gates live access behind KYC, and a demo has no live mode to gate.

## Conclusion

The market standard is less a feature list than a small set of rules applied everywhere. **Separate who grants access from who moves money. Make every money-affecting action reasoned, state-gated and recorded. Never mutate history. Make the environment impossible to confuse.** Enterprise tiers add scale to these rules (custom roles, SSO, organizations, SIEM feeds) but do not change them. payhub has already implemented the hardest parts in its backend: the append-only ledger, the governed `unknown` state and redelivery. So its UI can meet the market standard mostly by exposing those guarantees rather than inventing new ones. The two structural gaps are keys and approvals. A single digest column cannot support test/live separation or zero-downtime rotation, and an operator transition that one person can make alone falls short of the four-eyes control every regulated operator expects.

The largest uncertainty is internal consoles. No PSP documents theirs, so the operator-area recommendations rest on Monzo, Modern Treasury and Pigment rather than on the seven platforms studied. In that area payhub has an unusual opportunity: a public, working example of maker-checker transitions out of `unknown` and approved compensating ledger entries would show something the market leaders practise but do not publish.
